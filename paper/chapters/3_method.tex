\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We study residual vision networks as a three-part map.
The first part---stem, stage~1 and stage~2---encodes the image into a latent state
$x_0$; the second part---the deepest residual stage---transports $x_0$ to a state
$x_T$ ready for decoding; the third part---stage~4 and the prediction head---maps
$x_T$ to class logits.
We focus on that middle transport (stage~3 in ConvNeXt, Swin and ResNet). It holds
most of the residual depth, keeps a fixed representation and spatial resolution, and
is the stage with enough blocks to study dynamics: in ConvNeXt-T, for example,
stages~1,~2 and~4 have three blocks each, while stage~3 has nine. ConvNeXt inherits
this depth layout from Swin, itself patterned on ResNet, so the same middle stage is
the natural object of study in all three.

In ConvNeXt-T we take that stage's $n{=}9$ blocks as the trained horizon $T{=}n{=}9$,
and refine the discretisation with repetitions $R$ and step $\Delta t{=}1/R$
(\cref{sec:indefinite_looping}). Effective per-block step sizes may differ, but they
are absorbed into the learned weights, so integrating each block over one unit of time
is equivalent to the steps the model has already internalised.

The central question is functional: can a weight-tied stage~3---one shared residual
field reused over the horizon $T{=}n$---match the representational power of the
non-tied stage, which uses $n$ independent blocks and many more parameters?
Simply looping the blocks at finer step size does not answer this. Residual maps are
Lipschitz, so refining the discretisation at fixed $T$ always converges to a continuous
limit, trained or randomly initialised (\cref{app:random-init}); convergence alone is
therefore a weak claim.
What the shared construction does give us is a clean autonomous ODE for stage~3.
We then read the standard network the same way, block by block: each residual update
is a one-step Euler step of a local field, so the untied stage is a switched composition
of smooth ODE pieces rather than a single autonomous flow---hence the jumps in
$\|f\|$ at block boundaries in \cref{fig:teaser}.
Continuising both---tied and
untied---at inference yields high-resolution trajectories from $x_0$ to $x_T$, and those
trajectories are where the two models diverge: the standard path repeatedly redirects
and partially undoes itself, while the tied path stays closer to a monotone transport.
We organise the section accordingly:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \cref{sec:weight_tied} shows that a weight-tied stage~3 nearly matches an
    untied ConvNeXt-T ($80.36\%$ vs.\ $80.97\%$ top-1), so one shared field already
    carries most of the function;
    \item \cref{sec:indefinite_looping} continuises residual stages at inference without
    fine-tuning---ConvNeXt-T and its weight-tied variant, and also Swin-T and
    ResNet-50/101---showing the same pattern across architectures: at fixed $T$ every
    solver reaches one limit and retains almost all accuracy, whereas breaking $T$
    collapses the networks;
    \item \cref{sec:geometrical_analysis} compares the refined trajectories, autonomous
    (tied) versus switched (untied);
    \item \cref{sec:overparametrized_undoing} measures that pull-and-push along depth,
    and probes when stage~3 prefixes become usable by a frozen stage~4+head;
    \item \cref{sec:deltas} tests a hypothesis: small per-block corrections on a shared
    field as a way to recover what over-parameterisation wastes.
\end{enumerate}
